## 1. Minimum-distance / GMM estimation of covariance structures: moment selection, weighting, over-identification, standard errors, levels vs differences, reporting

### Takeaway
Equally weighted (identity or diagonal) minimum distance on the autocovariance function remains the default in this literature, for the small-sample-bias reason Altonji and Segal gave, so the program's identity-weighted NNLS is orthodox. Where current best practice goes beyond the program is in (a) treating the levels-versus-differences discrepancy as a standing misspecification test and tracing it to observations adjacent to gaps and spell boundaries in unbalanced panels, (b) Monte Carlo checks for weak identification of the permanent/persistent split when persistence is high relative to panel length, and (c) block-bootstrap rather than analytic inference.

### Cited Findings

**Weighting**
- Altonji and Segal's Monte Carlo study (using, among others, the Abowd–Card hours-and-earnings data) finds that optimally weighted minimum distance is seriously biased in small samples and remains biased in large samples for badly behaved distributions; the bias is almost always toward zero in absolute value and arises because sampling error in the second moments is correlated with sampling error in the weighting matrix. Equally weighted MD usually has lower RMSE and median absolute error; their split-sample "independently weighted" alternative removes the bias but is usually dominated by equal weighting. [AB] — [Altonji & Segal 1996, JBES 14(3):353–366; NBER Technical WP 156](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0156)
- Doris, O'Neill and Sweetman state that Altonji–Segal (1996) and Clark (1996) established the preference for the identity matrix in covariance-structure models, that this is now common practice, and they follow it; they give the sandwich asymptotic variance (G'G)^-1 G'SG (G'G)^-1 for the identity-weighted estimator. [FT] — [Doris, O'Neill & Sweetman 2010, Maynooth WP N208-10](https://repec.maynoothuniversity.ie/mayecw-files/N208-10.pdf); published as [J. Econ. Inequality 11:343–372 (2013), DOI 10.1007/s10888-012-9216-5](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/8692/1/s10888-012-9216-5.pdf)
- Moffitt and Gottschalk fit 1,197 age-group x year x lag autocovariance cells by identity-weighted MD, citing Altonji–Segal, with Chamberlain-type sandwich standard errors. [FT-s] — [Moffitt & Gottschalk 2012, J. Human Resources 47(1):204–236 (PMC author manuscript)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297675)
- Meghir and Pistaferri estimate the permanent-shock variance by equally weighted MD, again citing Altonji–Segal. [FT] — [Meghir & Pistaferri 2004, Econometrica 72(1):1–32, working-paper version](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3862/1/3862.pdf)
- In firm dynamics, Sterk, Sedlacek and Pugsley fit the upper triangle of the age-by-age autocovariance matrix of log employment by Chamberlain-style MD with equal weights, justified by the fact that every element is precisely estimated in Census-scale data. [FT] — [Pugsley, Sedlacek & Sterk, NBER conference draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f126347/f126347.pdf); published as [Sterk, Sedlacek & Pugsley 2021, AER 111(2):547–579](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20190748)
- The opposite choice appears when N is very large: Daly, Hryshko and Manovskii use the optimal weighting matrix (inverse covariance of the data moments) on Danish and German administrative samples of roughly 10,000–100,000 individuals, and estimate that matrix by block bootstrap in their simulated-MD extension. [FT] — [Daly, Hryshko & Manovskii, NBER WP 22938 (rev. May 2018)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938); published as IER 63(1):95–124 (2022)
- A recent proposal replaces the equal/diagonal convention: Cheng, Sanchez-Becerra and Shephard combine cross-fitting with a regularized weighting-matrix estimate and a cross-fitting standard error, argue that cross-fitting removes a first-order bias under many-moment asymptotics, and report earnings-dynamics simulations favouring it over common alternatives. Still a working paper (cemmap CWP13/23; presented at seminars through October 2025). [AB] — [Cheng, Sanchez-Becerra & Shephard 2023](https://economics.sas.upenn.edu/node/15540); [cemmap record](https://www.cemmap.ac.uk/?p=12275)
- Guvenen, Karahan, Ozkan and Song weight by judgment: the objective is a weighted sum of squared scaled arc-percent deviations, with stated relative weights on five moment groups; they report that equal weights across groups or across individual moments gave substantively similar estimates, and note that sufficiently different weights can always move estimates. [FT] — [GKOS, NY Fed Staff Report 710 (rev. Sept 2019)](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf); published as [Econometrica 89(5):2303–2339 (2021)](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2021/09/01/what-do-data-millions-us-workers-reveal-about-lifecycle)

**Identification and weak identification**
- In the model y_it = p_t*alpha_i + lambda_t*v_it with AR(1) v, the Jacobian column for the permanent variance is a vector of ones and the column for the initial transitory variance is powers of rho; as rho approaches one these become collinear and only the sum of the two variances is identified. Longer panels help because higher powers of rho separate the columns. [FT] — [Doris et al. 2010](https://repec.maynoothuniversity.ie/mayecw-files/N208-10.pdf)
- Monte Carlo (10,000 replications; N=2,000; T=8; rho=0.95): rho and the innovation variance are well estimated, but the permanent and initial-transitory variances are inconsistently estimated, analytic standard errors understate true dispersion, sampling distributions are skewed, a one-sided nominal 5% test has empirical size near zero, and about 2% of replications give a negative permanent variance. The authors conclude that negative variance estimates can signal weak identification rather than misspecification. With T=25 the problems largely disappear; with rho=0.8 and T=8 the model is well identified; serious problems arise only for rho at or above 0.95. [FT] — [Doris et al. 2010](https://repec.maynoothuniversity.ie/mayecw-files/N208-10.pdf)
- Time trends interact with persistence: identification improves when the transitory loading falls relative to the permanent loading and deteriorates when it rises. In a design with rho=0.8, T=8 and a rising transitory loading, about 1% of runs fail to converge, about 1% give negative transitory loadings, sampling distributions look bimodal, and nominal 5% tests have size 14–19%; N=10,000 does not cure it and N=40,000 still leaves non-normality. With rho at or below 0.6 the model is identified from eight periods for any plausible trend. [FT] — [Doris et al. 2010](https://repec.maynoothuniversity.ie/mayecw-files/N208-10.pdf)
- Moffitt and Gottschalk note that with an AR(1) transitory component the permanent variance is identified only by extrapolating the AR(1) decay to infinite lag, that estimates of rho near one are a recurring problem, and that the final-year permanent loading rests essentially on one observation. [FT-s] — [Moffitt & Gottschalk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297675)
- Sterk et al. make the same point constructively: with stationary components, the infinite-lag autocovariance is proportional to the variance of the permanent (ex-ante) level, so long-horizon autocovariances carry the information on permanent heterogeneity. [FT] — [Pugsley, Sedlacek & Sterk draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f126347/f126347.pdf)

**Levels versus differences**
- For a random walk plus i.i.d. transitory shock, DHM write the identifying moments in differences as sigma2_perm,t = sum over j in {-1,0,1} of E[dy_t dy_{t+j}] and sigma2_trans,t = -E[dy_t dy_{t+1}], and show the level-moment versions are algebraic expansions of the same expressions, so on a sample with no missing observations between t-2 and t+1 they must agree. A persistence-adjusted version (quasi-differences y_t - phi*y_{t-1}) covers phi < 1. [FT] — [DHM, NBER WP 22938](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- Empirically, in unbalanced samples the permanent-shock variance is about twice as large when targeting growth-rate moments and the transitory variance is larger when targeting level moments; in balanced samples both the difference-based permanent variance and the level-based transitory variance fall by more than half and the discrepancy nearly vanishes. [FT] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- Cause: each autocovariance is averaged over whoever contributes to it, and observations next to a missing record (start or end of a spell, or around an interior gap) are not random draws. In their data first-year earnings are about 0.5–0.6 log points below the individual's mean and last-year earnings 0.3–0.4 below; squared residuals in the first year are roughly 150% above typical; first/last-observation dummies explain 5–13% of residual variation. Modelled as an extra shock with negative mean mu and variance s2 hitting a share s_t of contributors, the bias is s_t*(mu^2 + s2), entering the transitory variance when levels are targeted and the permanent variance when differences are targeted. [FT] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- Quantification (German consecutive sample): permanent variance 0.013 (levels) versus 0.024 (differences), both 0.010 once gap-adjacent observations are netted out; transitory variance 0.020 (levels) versus 0.008 (differences), both 0.007 net. Dropping the first and last three observations of incomplete spells cuts the difference-based permanent variance by about 70% and the level-based transitory variance by about 60%. Dropping only one observation is not enough, because the next observation then becomes gap-adjacent. [FT] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- Their stated robustness ranking: the permanent variance is more robustly estimated from level moments, and the transitory variance (net of the boundary shock) from difference moments. The alternative fix is to model the boundary shock explicitly (mean, variance, MA persistence, by position: first, last, interior gap) and estimate by simulated MD; the extended model gives the same estimates from level or difference moments. Re-estimating with Student-t shocks (df about 4) left point estimates unchanged, since the discrepancy lives in second moments. [FT] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- What does not create a levels-differences wedge: omitted heterogeneous growth rates bias both versions of the permanent-variance moment equally (by three times the growth-rate variance in their example), as does correlation between initial conditions and permanent shocks. A different mechanism that can create a wedge is restricting heterogeneity in the persistence of the permanent component (credited to Hryshko and Manovskii 2017). Heathcote, Perri and Violante (2010) had read the discrepancy as evidence that the canonical process is misspecified. [FT, as reported by DHM] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- Moffitt and Gottschalk fit in levels because, once the permanent loading varies over time, the initial permanent level no longer differences out. [FT-s] — [Moffitt & Gottschalk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297675)

**Standard errors and tests**
- Meghir and Pistaferri compute all standard errors by a block bootstrap over individuals (Hall–Horowitz), which handles arbitrary serial correlation, heteroskedasticity and pre-estimated residuals; they note it uses only the N dimension. They choose the MA order with the Abowd–Card chi-square test of zero restrictions on higher-order autocovariances (one reported statistic is 279.83 on 253 df), use bootstrap p-values (300–1,000 replications) for chi-square tests and Sargan over-identification tests, and warn that padding a test with irrelevant instruments distorts it. [FT] — [Meghir & Pistaferri, WP version](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3862/1/3862.pdf)
- Parametric bootstrap is the norm for simulation estimators: GKOS simulate from the estimate, rebuild targets and re-estimate for 100 seeds; Arellano, Blundell and Bonhomme use the parametric bootstrap for pointwise bands. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf); [ABB, cemmap CWP53/15](https://www.ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/output_url_files/cwp531515.pdf)
- With aggregate shocks, inference from the cross-section alone is generally invalid and time dummies do not in general repair it; valid inference combines cross-section and time-series variation. [AB] — [Hahn, Kuersteiner & Mazzocco 2020, REStud 87:1365–1398 (arXiv 1507.04415)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.04415)
- Likelihood-based (Bayesian) estimators of these processes showed better small-sample bias and efficiency than GMM, including with time-varying and heterogeneous parameters, missing data, unbalanced panels and non-normal errors. [AB] — [Nakata & Tonetti 2015, J. Applied Economics 18:121–148](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/small-sample-properties-of-bayesian-estimators-of-labor-income-processes.htm)

**Moment selection and reporting**
- The convention is to target the entire autocovariance function in levels or first differences while proving identification from a small subset. Browning, Ejrnaes and Alvarez are named as the exception that adds other statistics. [FT] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- GKOS report a ladder of eight nested specifications with the objective value decomposed by moment group, plots of fit to targeted and non-targeted statistics, and release more than 10,000 empirical moments as a spreadsheet. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- Kaplan and Violante validate an estimator by applying it to data simulated from a structural model, showing the Blundell–Pistaferri–Preston estimator understates insurance against permanent shocks, more so when borrowing limits bind. [AB] — [Kaplan & Violante 2010, AEJ: Macro 2(4):53–87](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15553)
- Subsample composition matters for the same estimator on the same survey: Hryshko and Manovskii find sharply different income persistence across two PSID household groups and attribute much of it to differential attrition. [AB] — [Hryshko & Manovskii, "How much consumption insurance in the U.S.?"](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f99634.pdf)

### Inferences
- The program's identity-weighted exact NNLS on per-band moments, with a weighting audit that found nil effect, is consistent with mainstream practice and with the Sterk et al. rationale (precisely estimated moments make weighting second-order). A one-time sensitivity run with relative (percentage) deviations in the GKOS manner would address the remaining scale issue: within a band, identity weights on raw covariances let gamma_0 and D(52) dominate gamma_6.
- The program's moment set (change autocovariances plus long-difference variances) is entirely in the "differences" family. DHM imply two things for it: the difference-based permanent variance is the one exposed to upward bias from gap-adjacent observations, and a level-versus-difference comparison on a gap-free subsample is a free misspecification test that the program does not currently run. Long-difference variances are computed over entities present at both ends, so they inherit the same contributor-selection problem.
- NNLS hitting the zero bound in a band is the direct analogue of the negative-variance estimates Doris et al. diagnose as weak identification. The relevant quantity is panel length relative to the persistence timescale: their T=8, rho=0.95 failure has T at roughly 0.6 half-lives, their T=25 success about 1.9. If the home half-life is of order a year, an 88-week era is in the marginal zone and any shorter era split is in the failure zone. In an OU model with horizons up to 52 weeks, kappa is identified from the curvature of D(h), so (sigma_perm, kappa) lie on a ridge when 1/kappa is long compared with the horizon; a 4–8x era shift in kappa could be movement along that ridge.
- The MP remark that an entity bootstrap uses only the N dimension, together with Hahn–Kuersteiner–Mazzocco, supports the program's practice of pairing entity and week-block bootstraps; for claims about era trends, the time dimension (a handful of eras) is the binding sample size.

- I could not open the Cheng–Sanchez-Becerra–Shephard paper (HTTP 403), so the size of the gains and any published version are unverified.

## 2. Time-varying parameters: loading factors, separation from cohort/composition and life-cycle effects, parsimonious evolution and tests

### Takeaway
The literature's parsimonious device is a pair of period-specific loading factors (one on the permanent component, one on the transitory innovation), or period-specific variances, with identification coming from comparing the same age at different dates or from following cohorts; it comes with documented identification hazards at the sample edges and when persistence is high. A more recent alternative makes variances log-linear functions of time and age inside a state-space likelihood. The most relevant substantive precedent for the program's rising amplitude spread s is the finding that a rise in mean volatility was driven by the already most volatile units while the majority did not change.

### Cited Findings
- Moffitt and Gottschalk's specification: random walk plus random growth for the permanent component, ARMA(1,1) transitory component with age-linear innovation variance, a calendar loading alpha_t multiplying the permanent component and beta_t multiplying the transitory innovation, common across ages; calendar time is deliberately allowed to shift the model in only those two places. Calendar effects are identified by comparing variances at the same age across years; a separate parameter handles the unobserved pre-sample transitory history. [FT-s] — [Moffitt & Gottschalk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297675)
- They document why shortcut methods mislead: subtracting a long-lag autocovariance from the variance yields a spurious fall in transitory variance when the permanent loading is rising, because the lagged covariance carries the product of two loadings; the repair is a regression of log autocovariances at lags of ten or more on year dummies for both dates plus a low-order polynomial in age and lag. Window-averaging methods are consistent only without transitory serial correlation and blur turning points. Results were checked against trimming (none, 1%, 5% per tail), residualization choices and a full-year-worker restriction. [FT-s] — [Moffitt & Gottschalk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297675)
- The Doris–O'Neill–Sweetman Stata command estimates random-walk or random-growth permanent components and AR(1)/ARMA(1,1) transitory components with time and cohort loadings on both; first-period loadings are normalized to one. Their Monte Carlo shows that trends in the loadings can either rescue or destroy identification (see section 1). [FT for the model and Monte Carlo; AB for the command] — [Doris et al. 2010](https://repec.maynoothuniversity.ie/mayecw-files/N208-10.pdf); [Stata Journal 11(3):439–459](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/196680/files/sjart_st0237.pdf)
- Baker and Solon, with Canadian tax records and men with at least nine consecutive positive-earnings years, find both heterogeneous growth and a random walk significant, and report that the data reject several restrictions commonly imposed in the U.S. literature, with distorted inference if they are imposed. Haider uses a heterogeneous-growth model on the PSID and finds the persistent component only mildly countercyclical and instability strongly so. [AB] — [Baker & Solon 2003, J. Labor Economics 21:289–321 (working paper)](https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/public/workingPapers/UT-ECIPA-BAKER-98-01.pdf); [Haider 2001, J. Labor Economics 19(4):799–836](https://www.rand.org/pubs/drafts/DRU2375.html)
- DeBacker et al. run an error-components model alongside simpler non-model decompositions on a tax-return panel and report that the trends in permanent and transitory variances agree closely across methods. [AB] — [DeBacker, Heim, Panousi, Ramnath & Vidangos 2013, BPEA](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/rising-inequality-transitory-or-persistent-new-evidence-from-a-panel-of-u-s-tax-returns); [FEDS 2011-60](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2011/201160/index.html)
- Composition can be removed by design: DHM restrict to a single five-year birth cohort so that cohort effects need not be modelled; Blundell, Graber and Mogstad use population data to allow non-stationarity in both age and calendar time within education groups. [FT for DHM; AB for BGM] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938); [Blundell, Graber & Mogstad 2015, J. Public Economics 127:58–73](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1432915)
- Meghir and Pistaferri put year effects directly in the conditional variances of permanent and transitory shocks and note that covariance stationarity is often rejected in PSID earnings. [FT] — [Meghir & Pistaferri, WP version](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3862/1/3862.pdf)
- Braxton, Herkenhoff, Rothbaum and Schmidt estimate a persistent-plus-temporary state-space model by Kalman filter, smoother and EM. Shock variances are log-linear in observables (age, calendar time, employment status, occupation), which keeps the M-step concave with closed-form GLS-like updates; they describe this variance modelling as new. They argue it lets them track heterogeneity in the evolution of risk in a way GMM approaches typically do not, describe the approach as combining year-specific risk (as in Blundell–Pistaferri–Preston) with life-cycle variation (as in Karahan–Ozkan), and use five-year windows where cells are thin. [FT] — [Braxton et al., NBER WP 29567 (rev. Dec 2023)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29567/w29567.pdf); published as [AER 115(12):4438–75 (2025)](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20231656)
- Jensen and Shore find no systematic rise in volatility for the large majority of individuals; the rise in average volatility came almost entirely from those with the most volatile earnings, identifiable in advance from large past changes. The estimator is their nonparametric Bayesian stochastic-volatility model. [AB] — [Jensen & Shore 2015, J. Human Resources 50(3):811–836](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/50/3/811)
- Measured volatility trends are sensitive to data handling: a multi-dataset reconciliation finds little long-run trend once differences are accounted for, and attributes most remaining cross-dataset differences to the left tail of the cross-sectional distribution. [AB] — [Moffitt et al. 2023, JBES 41(1):1–11 (arXiv 2202.00713)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00713)
- Almuzara, Arellano, Blundell and Bonhomme let the persistent component of a nonlinear micro income process depend on a macro state with its own law of motion, combining macro time series with a time series of short panels; they find the cycle changes persistence and risk. [AB] — [NY Fed Staff Report 1162 (Aug 2025, rev. Oct 2025)](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1162.pdf)

### Inferences
- The fact sheet lists a common time-varying volatility factor as refuted. A common transitory loading beta_t is that object, so it should not be re-proposed. What the loading-factor literature does not rule out, and Jensen–Shore positively suggest, is time variation in the dispersion of volatility across entities. The one-parameter-per-era version is an exponent on log amplitude: log v_it = lambda_t * log v_i, with lambda normalized to one in the base era. It preserves b=1 within an era, makes s_t = lambda_t * s, and is testable by a restricted-versus-free distance comparison.
- Before any such parameter is added, the literature's first move is to hold composition fixed. The program's universe is defined on full-window time-averaged rank, so its membership is not era-neutral: entities that enter, grow or fade inside the window contribute gap-adjacent and low-level (high-variance) observations to some eras and not others. The earnings analogue of age is time since first appearance; the analogue of cohort is entry period. The s trend should be recomputed on a balanced, gap-trimmed entity set and by entry cohort before it is interpreted as calendar-time change.
- Jensen–Shore give a sharp diagnostic for which kind of change it is: track quantiles of per-entity change variance by era. If the median and lower quantiles are flat and only the top decile rises, the trend is a tail phenomenon (risk types), not a rescaling of the lognormal.
- The Moffitt–Gottschalk warning about lagged covariances under changing loadings applies to any moment that spans an era boundary, including 52-week long differences: such moments mix two eras' loadings and will bias kappa if eras are fitted separately with boundary-spanning long differences included.
- Inference on an era trend with three or four eras is a time-series problem. Entity-level standard errors will make almost any difference look significant; the honest unit is the era or the week block.
- For forecasting (the under-dispersed p90), a likelihood-based filter with log-linear variance terms in time is the literature's route to letting variances drift without a parameter per era; rolling re-estimation appears only as windowing where cells are thin.

### Gaps
- I found no use of exponential forgetting or discounted estimation in the sources reviewed; I did not get to run a dedicated search for it.
- No source reviewed gives a formal test for time variation in the cross-sectional dispersion of volatility in a method-of-moments setting; the test proposed above is my construction.
- The Baker–Solon cohort-loading specification and the Blundell–Pistaferri–Preston year-specific variance estimator were not read in the original.
- The Moffitt–Gottschalk 1995 working paper (the origin of the loading factors) was not retrieved.

## 3. Heterogeneity in variances and persistence: ARCH versus persistent heterogeneity, common versus separate multipliers, NPMLE of the amplitude distribution

### Takeaway
The literature offers a clean moment-based test that separates permanent unobserved heterogeneity in variances from state dependence (ARCH), separately for permanent and transitory shocks, and that is robust to serially correlated measurement error; with T of 88–213 it would be far better powered here than in the 9–26-year panels it was built for. Nonparametric maximum likelihood for the distribution of individual variances is established, convex, and implemented in software; it is best used first as a diagnostic of the lognormal assumption with proper deconvolution of sampling noise.

### Cited Findings
- Meghir and Pistaferri specify ARCH(1) conditional variances with year effects and an individual fixed effect, separately for the transitory and the permanent shock. Because the two shocks are not separately observed, they work with products of earnings-growth residuals: a growth cross-product at lag q+1 is proportional to the squared transitory shock, and growth times a symmetric window of leads and lags of growth has conditional expectation equal to the squared permanent shock. [FT] — [Meghir & Pistaferri, WP version](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3862/1/3862.pdf)
- The ARCH coefficients are identified knowing only the MA order, and remain identified with classical or serially correlated measurement error provided the error has no ARCH component. First-differencing out the variance fixed effect gives weak instruments, so they use within-group demeaning plus instruments lagged two periods, relying on T between 9 and 26 to keep within-group bias small. [FT] — [Meghir & Pistaferri, WP version](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3862/1/3862.pdf)
- The heterogeneity test compares the ARCH coefficient estimated in levels with the within-group estimate, with bootstrap critical values; they find unobserved heterogeneity in at least one of the two variances in every education group, and pooled ARCH coefficients of about 0.40 (transitory) and 0.56 (permanent). Asymmetric ARCH responses are not identifiable because the two shocks are not separately observed. They note that a stochastic-volatility term would be detectable only if sufficiently serially correlated. [FT] — [Meghir & Pistaferri, WP version](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3862/1/3862.pdf)
- Hospido models linear individual effects in the mean and multiplicative individual effects in an ARCH-type variance, with a bias-corrected likelihood that reduces fixed-T bias to order 1/T^2; an early draft reports that ARCH effects vanish in a sample without job changes, and the published abstract attributes variance dynamics to job mobility. [AB] — [Hospido 2012, J. Applied Econometrics 27(3):386–414 (CEMFI WP 0717)](https://www.cemfi.es/ftp/wp/0717.pdf)
- Browning, Ejrnaes and Alvarez let most parameters of an ARMA-type process differ across individuals and find much more latent heterogeneity than earlier work; a later paper counts 44 targeted moments. [AB] — [Browning, Ejrnaes & Alvarez 2010, REStud 77:1353–1381](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v77y2010i4p1353-1381.html)
- GKOS report that adding ex-ante heterogeneity of that kind did not improve fit to their targeted moments; in their model, shock probabilities that vary with age and the persistent state already generate heterogeneity in innovation variances, which they liken to ARCH effects. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- Jensen and Shore build a semiparametric Bayesian stochastic-volatility model with a Markovian hierarchical Dirichlet-process prior, allowing volatility to differ across people and evolve over time; they find both. [AB] — [Jensen & Shore 2011, JASA 106:1280–1290 (PARC WP 12-03)](https://repository.upenn.edu/parc_working_papers/37)
- Gu and Koenker treat the individual intercept and the innovation variance as jointly random with an unrestricted distribution estimated by the Kiefer–Wolfowitz NPMLE, cast as a convex program; the AR(1) parameter is profiled. With variance heterogeneity allowed, persistence is modest (about 0.48), and intercepts and variances are negatively dependent, which they say matters for forecasting. The routine is in the R package REBayes (weighted longitudinal Gaussian model with dependent mean and variance components). [AB] — [Gu & Koenker 2017, JBES 35(1):1–16](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/jnlbes/v35y2017i1p1-16.html); [cemmap CWP43/14](https://cemmap.ac.uk/?p=9645); [REBayes WGLVmix](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/REBayes/versions/2.60/topics/WGLVmix)
- A January 2026 preprint extends this to nonparametric priors over intercepts, slopes, autoregressive coefficients and error variances in short panels, notes that the original conditions on the initial observation and assumes a common AR coefficient, and reports that empirical-Bayes estimates beat individual maximum likelihood on mean squared prediction error. [AB] — [Song, Lee & Ng 2026, arXiv 2601.07059](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07059)
- Almuzara allows a household-specific transitory variance that may be correlated with permanent income, gives nonparametric identification, and finds sizable, highly asymmetric heterogeneity in transitory risk and a negative, nonlinear relation with permanent income. A later paper states that such heterogeneity needs at least five periods where the canonical model needs three. [AB; 2nd] — [Almuzara 2020, seminar abstract](https://www.cerge-ei.cz/macro/2020-01-10/martin-almuzara); [Almuzara et al., SR 1162](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr1162.pdf)
- Botosaru and Sasaki identify and estimate nonparametrically the skedastic function of innovations to a unit-root latent state (the Stata package needs three periods of data), and find more earnings risk for those with lower prior permanent earnings in recent recessions. [AB] — [Botosaru & Sasaki 2018, J. Econometrics 203(2):283–296](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/econom/v203y2018i2p283-296.html); [NPSS](https://ideas.repec.org/c/boc/bocode/s458772.html)
- Arellano and Bonhomme show that variances of individual-specific coefficients are identified in fixed-T panels only under restricted error dependence, a trade-off between heterogeneity and error dynamics. [AB] — [Arellano & Bonhomme 2012, REStud 79(3):987–1020](https://www.cemfi.es/~arellano/Random11.pdf)
- Heterogeneous profiles: omitting individual-specific growth inflates estimated persistence (about 0.8 with heterogeneous profiles versus 0.99 without), and average-autocovariance tests have little power against that alternative; using growth-rate moments, the growth-rate variance is estimated at zero; and age variation in the covariance structure can be rationalized by age-dependent heteroskedasticity, which biases heterogeneous-profile estimates upward. In GKOS, adding heterogeneous profiles lowered persistence from 0.983 to 0.958 and improved fit to impulse-response moments. [AB for the first three; FT for GKOS] — [Guvenen 2009, RED 12(1):58–79](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13394); [Hryshko 2012, QE 3(2):177–209](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/quantitative-economics/2012/07/01/Labor-income-profiles-are-not-heterogeneous-Evidence-from-income-growth-rates); [Hoffmann 2019, QE](https://mail.econometricsociety.org/publications/quantitative-economics/2019/07/01/HIP-RIP-and-the-robustness-of-empirical-earnings-processes/file/QE863.pdf); [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- Under heavy tails, moment-based risk measures such as the variance may not exist; conditional Pareto exponents have been proposed instead. [AB; arXiv identifier taken from a search listing, not opened] — [Sarpietro, Sasaki & Wang, arXiv 2203.08014](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2203.08014)

### Inferences
- **Heterogeneity versus ARCH/stochastic volatility.** The MP design transfers directly: build the two cross-product proxies from weekly changes (using the program's fitted fast-AR order in place of the MA order), regress each on its own lag with week effects, once pooled and once within entity. Equality of the two slopes means no persistent heterogeneity; a surviving within-entity slope means state dependence. The program's long T removes the within-group bias that constrained MP. The proxies are robust to serially correlated measurement error, which the noise-floor pin is not.
- **One multiplier versus two.** Entity-level time averages of the two proxies are noisy estimates of the entity's permanent-shock and transitory-shock variances. Regressing one on the other in logs with a split-sample instrument (odd weeks instrumenting even weeks) gives a slope that estimates b and a disattenuated correlation that says whether one amplitude suffices. This is a different design from the program's existing b measurement and would be an independent replication of the b=1 law, or a refutation.
- **Finite-T inflation of s.** If s is read from the cross-entity dispersion of log sample variances, sampling noise in each sample variance adds to that dispersion, and the addition grows with kurtosis and volatility clustering and with shorter windows. If the existing estimator does not deconvolve this, shorter or more leptokurtic eras would show larger s mechanically. NPMLE (or any method that uses the sampling distribution of the entity statistic) removes this; so does the split-sample covariance of log variances.
- **Is NPMLE of the amplitude distribution worthwhile?** As a diagnostic, yes: it answers directly whether the upper tail of amplitude is lognormal, which is the mechanism the fact sheet implicates in R2 (lognormal amplitude times Student-t at the head), and it does so per platform, which fits the sign reversal between Facebook and Reddit submissions. As a replacement for the lognormal in the frozen model, only if the diagnostic rejects and the estimated mixing distribution can be summarized in one or two parameters; the NPMLE itself is discrete and would add to the parsimony liability. Transfer caveats: the Gu–Koenker likelihood is Gaussian given the individual variance, assumes a common AR coefficient and conditions on the first observation; here shocks are Student-t and variances depend on rank band, so entity statistics must be standardized by the band profile and the sampling distribution adjusted for kurtosis and serial dependence, or a quantile-based scale used instead.
- **Dependence of amplitude on level.** Gu–Koenker and Almuzara both find variance heterogeneity negatively related to the permanent level. In the program the sigma(z) profiles absorb level dependence on average, but residual dependence of v_i on home level within a band is testable and bears on the head law.
- **kappa_i heterogeneity (R6).** The 2026 empirical-Bayes result suggests the reason heterogeneous reversion rates did not predict out of sample may be that they were used unshrunk; shrinkage toward the band value is the form in which such heterogeneity has been shown to help prediction. This is worth one registered test, not more.
- **Drift heterogeneity as a confound for kappa.** The profile-heterogeneity debate says slow reversion and heterogeneous trends are hard to separate from average autocovariances, and that profile flexibility in variances can masquerade as either. The program's refutation of constant-drift demeaning matches Hryshko's growth-rate result; the residual lesson is that band-flexible variance profiles can absorb misspecification elsewhere, so over-identification tests should be run with profiles restricted (section 7).

- Alvarez, Browning & Ejrnaes (the companion heterogeneity paper) and Chamberlain & Hirano (1999) were not retrieved.
- I could not read the Gu–Koenker, Jensen–Shore, Almuzara or Botosaru–Sasaki full texts; mechanics beyond the abstracts are unverified, including the shape of any estimated variance distribution.
- I found no paper in this literature that tests a single common volatility multiplier against separate permanent and transitory multipliers; MP estimate separate fixed effects but do not report their correlation in the passages I read. The split-sample test above is my construction.
- I found no source that separates stochastic volatility from ARCH beyond the MP remark on detectability.

## 4. Non-Gaussian and nonlinear dynamics, estimation side

### Takeaway
Two estimation templates dominate. The simulated-method-of-moments template targets quantile-based higher-order moments of short and long differences and conditional impulse responses, by cells of a predetermined permanent-income measure, with simulated data passed through the same sample rules as the real data. The latent-variable template alternates draws of the latent persistent component with quantile regressions. Both validate on statistics that a covariance matrix cannot see, and the first reports which component carries the non-Gaussianity.

### Cited Findings
- GKOS process: an AR(1) persistent component with two-normal-mixture innovations, a two-normal-mixture transitory shock, a nonemployment shock whose probability is a logistic function of age and the persistent state, and optionally heterogeneous profiles. Normal mixtures are chosen for flexibility and because they add little computational burden. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- Targets: five moment sets, 1,196 moments in all: standard deviation, skewness and kurtosis of one- and five-year arc-percent earnings growth by age and recent-earnings cells; impulse-response moments; average earnings by lifetime-earnings group over the life cycle; the distribution of years employed; and the age profile of within-cohort log-earnings variance. Quantile-based measures (Kelley skewness, Crow–Siddiqui kurtosis) are used alongside standardized moments. Impulse responses are computed between t+k and t-1 so that the set of workers is constant across horizons. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- Conditioning: individuals are grouped by recent earnings, a residualized average of earlier years' earnings, i.e. a predetermined permanent-income proxy; the authors stress that sign- and size-dependent mean reversion is hidden in a covariance matrix. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- Objective: each deviation is an arc-percent difference between simulated and data moment with an additive adjustment in the denominator (the 10th percentile of absolute moments in the set) so near-zero moments do not dominate; 100,000 simulated histories; simulated data pass through exactly the same selection criteria and construction as the real data; a multi-start global search from about 250,000 quasi-random points taking days on a cluster. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- Findings about estimation: a linear-Gaussian process forced onto these moments returns implausible parameters (persistence above one, very large transitory and fixed-effect dispersion), traced to a single moment set; uniform transitory disaster shocks add kurtosis but not skewness; non-Gaussianity is found mainly in the persistent innovations (skewness about -1.1, kurtosis about 8 in the benchmark), and allowing a non-Gaussian transitory shock changes little; large changes followed by partial reversion require both mixtures and state-dependent nonemployment risk. A two-state version (persistences 0.98 and 0.82) fits better. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- ABB model: a persistent first-order Markov component with a general conditional quantile function plus a transitory component, specified with low-order Hermite polynomial bases on an 11-point quantile grid, interpolated between grid points, with exponential tails beyond the extreme quantiles. Identification follows hidden-Markov arguments (Hu–Schennach; Wilhelm) and needs at least four periods to identify one Markov transition. [FT] — [ABB, cemmap CWP53/15](https://www.ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/output_url_files/cwp531515.pdf); published as [Econometrica 85(3):693–734 (2017)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1560508/)
- ABB algorithm: a stochastic EM in the Celeux–Diebolt sense that is not likelihood-based in the M-step. The E-step draws the latent persistent path by random-walk Metropolis–Hastings (target acceptance about 30%); the M-step is a set of quantile regressions on the draws. They use one draw per iteration, 500 iterations of 200 Metropolis steps for the earnings parameters, average the last half of the chain, restart from many initial values and keep the run with the highest average likelihood because the chain can get stuck. The estimator is root-N consistent and asymptotically normal for fixed basis dimension under correct specification; inference is by parametric bootstrap. [FT] — [ABB, cemmap CWP53/15](https://www.ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/output_url_files/cwp531515.pdf)
- ABB validation: persistence measured as the derivative of the conditional quantile function, and conditional skewness, are computed on the data and on model-simulated data and compared, with bootstrap bands; they report that an alternative in the Meghir–Pistaferri mould does not appear able to reproduce the nonlinear asymmetric patterns (I saw only the end of that sentence, so the exact comparator is unconfirmed). The PSID sample is a balanced panel of 792 households; the Norwegian comparison sample is about 790,000. [FT] — [ABB, cemmap CWP53/15](https://www.ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/output_url_files/cwp531515.pdf)
- De Nardi, Fella and Paz-Pardo estimate a nonparametric Markov chain: age-specific transition matrices between percentile-rank bins, with each bin's value set to its mean, optionally of higher order; the 2020 paper compares it with the canonical linear process. [AB; the binning description is from the authors' 2016 working paper] — [De Nardi, Fella & Paz-Pardo 2020, JEEA 18(2):890–926](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24326)
- Hu, Moffitt and Sasaki identify the full distributions of permanent and transitory components semiparametrically and find both more dispersed, skewed and fat-tailed than normal, with the transitory specification materially changing the inferred permanent distribution. [AB] — [Hu, Moffitt & Sasaki 2019, QE 10(4):1495–1536](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/quantitative-economics/2019/11/01/Semiparametric-estimation-of-the-canonical-permanenttransitory-model-of-earnings-dynamics)
- The Kalman-filter/EM estimator of Braxton et al. is quasi-maximum-likelihood and is reported to recover individual persistent earnings with bias under 0.1% when innovations are drawn from the GKOS mixtures. [FT] — [Braxton et al., NBER WP 29567](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29567/w29567.pdf)
- GRID applies one master code across 13 countries to produce harmonized statistics by year, age, gender and a permanent-income measure, including higher-order moments and mobility measures. [AB] — [Guvenen, Pistaferri & Violante 2022, QE 13(4):1321–1360](https://www.econometricsociety.org/doi/10.3982/QE2260)

### Inferences
- The program places its heavy tails in the fast transitory innovations and has refuted several modifications of that component. GKOS found, in a structurally similar decomposition, that the non-Gaussianity sits mainly in the persistent innovations and that transitory fat tails add kurtosis without reproducing large-change-then-partial-reversion patterns. The horizon profile of quantile kurtosis discriminates: fat tails confined to a fast component wash out as the differencing horizon grows; fat tails in a persistent or medium-timescale component do not. This is a measurement on existing data, by band, at h = 1, 4, 13, 52.
- The GKOS impulse-response moments are the model-free form of the R1 diagnosis (large persistent displacement followed by return). Computing mean subsequent change conditional on the size and sign of an initial change, by permanent-rank band, with constant composition across horizons, would show whether established entities' large negative moves revert faster than kappa implies and slower than the fast AR implies. The ABB quantile-autoregression derivative is the continuous version.
- GKOS condition on a predetermined average; the program conditions on a full-window average. For variance moments this matters little, but for conditional impulse responses a full-window average that includes the post-shock period mechanically builds in reversion toward it. Impulse-response moments should be conditioned on a lagged-window permanent rank.
- The program estimates Student-t degrees of freedom from within-entity kurtosis. With df near 4 (the value DHM found for earnings) the population kurtosis is infinite and its sample analogue is unstable; a quantile-based tail measure is the literature's replacement.
- The ABB stochastic-EM machinery is expensive and designed for short panels with unrestricted nonlinearity. With T of 88–213 the program can measure nonlinear persistence nearly directly after filtering; I would treat ABB as a source of validation statistics, not as an estimator to adopt. GKOS-style SMM is the closer fit, but its multi-day global search over a dozen parameters is the cost of abandoning the program's closed-form moment map.

### Gaps
- Arellano, Blundell, Bonhomme & Light (2023/2024, heterogeneity of consumption responses under nonlinear persistence) could not be searched before the budget ran out; nothing is asserted about it.
- I read the GKOS staff-report revision and the ABB cemmap working paper, not the published Econometrica versions.
- The De Nardi–Fella–Paz-Pardo grid size and treatment of the transitory component are unverified.
- Altonji, Smith & Vidangos (2013, indirect inference) and Busch, Domeij, Guvenen & Madera (2022) were not retrieved; the GRID country papers in the QE special issue were not read.

## 5. Measurement error: identification from replicated or validation measures, pitfalls, sensitivity analyses

### Takeaway
Validation studies repeatedly find that measurement error is not classical: it is negatively related to the true value, serially correlated, and present in the supposedly true record as well. Without external information the measurement-error variance is not separately identified from the transitory variance, and the literature handles that with bounds or an external ratio, not a point pin. For a noise floor built from within-week daily replication, the specific additional hazard is time aggregation.

### Cited Findings
- The handbook review tests the classical assumptions (error uncorrelated with the truth, with other variables and with other errors) against validation evidence and frequently finds errors negatively correlated with true values; it recommends randomly sampled validation studies and public validation microdata. [AB] — [Bound, Brown & Mathiowetz 2001, Handbook of Econometrics vol. 5, ch. 59](https://ideas.repec.org/h/eee/ecochp/5-59.html)
- Gottschalk and Huynh, linking survey to tax records, find mean-reverting error whose effects on a mobility measure (the intertemporal correlation) largely offset, so survey and administrative correlations are similar while survey inequality is understated; their framework allows errors correlated across the two periods. [AB] — [Gottschalk & Huynh 2010, REStat 92(2):302–315 (IZA DP 2327)](https://repec.iza.org/dp2327.pdf)
- Kapteyn and Ypma were the first to allow error in both the survey and the administrative source, using a mixture; once administrative error (for example mismatched records) is modelled, the mean reversion usually blamed on the survey nearly disappears. The mixture depends on an assumed error-free share, and later work finds estimates not fully robust to it. [2nd] — [Jenkins & Rios-Avila, IZA DP 13196](https://docs.iza.org/dp13196.pdf); [Stata conference abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/boc/scon21/33.html)
- Abowd and Stinson treat every source as a noisy measure, define the truth as a weighted combination with weights that must be set a priori absent a truth audit, and find similar reliability for survey and administrative earnings except where survey months were imputed. [AB] — [Abowd & Stinson 2013, REStat 95(5):1451–1467](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v95y2013i5p1451-1467.html)
- Meghir and Pistaferri state that the transitory variance, the measurement-error variance and the MA coefficient cannot be separately identified from growth autocovariances (two equations, three unknowns). They either bound the parameters (the requirement that the implied error variance be non-negative bounds the MA coefficient) or import an external reliability figure, taking 25% of growth-rate variance as error, between two validation-study values of 28% and 22%. Both routes assume classical error, an assumption they flag as contested; their ARCH estimates do not depend on it. [FT] — [Meghir & Pistaferri, WP version](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3862/1/3862.pdf)
- Time aggregation: summing a random walk over a period induces serial correlation in the changes of the aggregate that the underlying series does not have; ignoring it materially changed a well-known estimate (a partial-insurance coefficient moved from about 0.05 to about 0.24). A later paper estimates income processes allowing for time-aggregated observation. [AB] — [Crawley 2020, Economics Letters 189 (FEDS 2019-075)](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/in-search-of-lost-time-aggregation.htm)
- Partial-period observation is a level-dependent, non-classical error: DHM attribute most of the low level and high variance of first and last spell observations to working less than the full period. [FT] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- Nonclassical error with repeated measurements is nonparametrically identifiable under conditional-independence conditions; this is the result the ABB identification argument builds on. [2nd, via ABB] — [ABB, cemmap CWP53/15](https://www.ifs.org.uk/sites/default/files/output_url_files/cwp531515.pdf)
- Moffitt and Gottschalk trim 1% of residuals per tail within cells and show that trimming choices affect late-sample trends. [FT-s] — [Moffitt & Gottschalk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297675)

### Inferences
- The program's noise-floor pin is an external-estimate strategy in the MP sense, and it shares the assumption MP flag: that the error is classical. Four things the validation literature would ask of it:
  1. **Is the replicate dispersion noise or signal?** Within-week daily dispersion contains real within-week dynamics (the fast AR component moves daily) and day-of-week seasonality as well as noise. A pin that counts these as noise over-allocates to the white component and under-allocates to the fast AR component, which changes implied gamma_1 and the short-horizon variance ratio.
  2. **Is the error white across weeks?** A split-half design tests it: build two weekly series from disjoint day sets (odd and even days). Their difference removes the common weekly signal; its week-to-week autocovariance should be zero under the pin. Its variance as a function of level tests heteroskedasticity (count-type shot noise predicts decline with level), and its correlation with the level tests mean reversion.
  3. **Is the record itself errorful?** The Kapteyn–Ypma lesson is that the administrative source has its own error process (mismatch, missing records). CrowdTangle eras and the Instagram query censoring are that kind of error, level-dependent and persistent; a daily-replication floor cannot see an error that is common to all days of a week.
  4. **Time aggregation.** Weekly totals of a process that moves within the week have positively autocorrelated changes and a compressed change variance relative to point-sampled values (Working's effect). A discrete weekly AR(1) plus white noise fitted to gamma_0..gamma_6 will absorb that into phi, sigma_trans and sigma_obs. The daily panel allows a direct check: compare the first-order autocorrelation of weekly changes computed from weekly totals with that from a fixed weekday's value.
- Recommended sensitivity battery, in the bounds spirit of MP and the prior-weights spirit of Abowd–Stinson: re-estimate with the noise variance set to 0, 0.5, 1 and 1.5 times the floor and report how the scorecard, the gate and the head law move; report the admissible range of the noise variance implied by non-negativity of the other components per band.
- Gottschalk–Huynh's offsetting result is a caution for the validation hierarchy: mobility statistics (the gate's rank-displacement moments) can be nearly immune to non-classical error while concentration statistics (the head law, top-1 share) are not. A measurement-regime explanation for R2 with an unaffected gate is therefore coherent, and the sign reversal between a censored-sample platform and a census platform is what it would predict.

### Gaps
- Bound & Krueger (1991) and Hu & Schennach (2008) were not read directly. The Kapteyn–Ypma and Abowd–Stinson papers were seen only through abstracts and later authors.
- The 0.25 first-order autocorrelation of changes in a time-averaged continuous random walk is the textbook Working value; I saw it confirmed only in a secondary document, not in Crawley's text.
- I found no source on measurement error arising from aggregating daily to weekly data in a replication-based noise estimate specifically; items 1–4 are inferences.
- The handbook chapter's treatment of autocorrelated errors and reliability in first differences could not be retrieved.

## 6. Attrition, entry/exit and unbalanced panels; scoring simulated and empirical moments under identical conditioning

### Takeaway
Selection on shocks around entry, exit and gaps is real, large, and biases variance estimates in a known direction; the literature's remedies are to trim or model gap-adjacent observations, to test sensitivity on long-stayers, and, in both the earnings-SMM and firm-dynamics traditions, to pass simulated data through the same selection and construction rules as the empirical data before computing moments.

### Cited Findings
- DHM conclude that attrition effects on the earnings autocovariance function, previously described as little understood, can be large; missing records are preceded by negative and followed by positive growth, though observables predict the incidence of missing records only weakly, in line with earlier PSID attrition analysis. About 23% and 31% of individuals in the German consecutive sample start after the first year or end before the last. [FT] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- The DHM remedies are trimming three observations on each side of a gap or modelling the boundary shock by position with simulated MD on data that replicate the unbalanced structure; a simulation with the same person-year structure but no boundary shock shows no levels-differences discrepancy, and adding the boundary shock reproduces it. [FT] — [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)
- Meghir and Pistaferri assume attrition depends only on fixed characteristics (removed by differencing and within-group demeaning), not on shocks, and test this by re-estimating on individuals observed 16 or more years; they report some differences. [FT] — [Meghir & Pistaferri, WP version](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3862/1/3862.pdf)
- Moffitt and Gottschalk compute each autocovariance on individuals observed at both dates in an unbalanced panel and flag attrition effects on autocovariances as poorly understood. [FT-s] — [Moffitt & Gottschalk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297675)
- Abowd, Crepon, Kramarz and Trognon decompose firm-panel autocovariances into permanent heterogeneity, unknown-age history differences and shock dynamics while modelling endogenous firm death in an unbalanced panel; for employment, compensation and capital, initial heterogeneity and random growth dominate. [AB] — [NBER Technical WP 180 (1995)](https://www.nber.org/papers/t0180)
- Sterk et al. compute autocovariances separately on a balanced panel of firms surviving to age 19–20 and on the unbalanced panel of all firms. Exit affects essentially only cross-sectional dispersion by age; autocorrelations are very similar across the two. The reduced-form model is fitted to the balanced panel. [FT] — [Pugsley, Sedlacek & Sterk draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f126347/f126347.pdf)
- Their structural model, with endogenous entry and exit, is then fitted to the same balanced-panel autocovariance matrix plus the age profiles of exit and average size from the unbalanced panel (249 moments, 10 parameters), so model and data are conditioned on survival identically. Selection makes the ex-ante and ex-post components negatively correlated among survivors, and the authors report a band for the variance decomposition that brackets how the covariance term is attributed. [FT] — [Pugsley, Sedlacek & Sterk draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f126347/f126347.pdf)
- GKOS apply identical selection criteria and construction to simulated and actual data, target arc-percent changes so that exits to zero stay in the sample, and compute impulse responses on a fixed set of workers across horizons. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- Braxton et al. treat income during unemployment as missing, let the latent persistent state keep evolving with its own drift and variance during the spell, and identify that law of motion from earnings on re-entry. [FT] — [Braxton et al., NBER WP 29567](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29567/w29567.pdf)
- Hryshko and Manovskii attribute large differences in estimated income persistence across PSID subsamples substantially to differential attrition. [AB] — [Hryshko & Manovskii](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f99634.pdf)

### Inferences
- The program scores under matched conditioning but estimates through an analytic moment map that has no selection in it. Long-difference variances D(h) are averaged over entities present at both ends; entities that suffered a large negative displacement are less likely to be present (or inside the universe) at the far end, so long-horizon dispersion is survivor-truncated in exactly the tail that R1 is about, and the truncation grows with h, distorting the curvature that identifies kappa. Estimating by simulated minimum distance with the empirical presence rule applied to simulated panels (the DHM and Sterk practice) removes the inconsistency between how the model is estimated and how it is scored.
- The DHM boundary shock is an estimation-side description of what the fact sheet calls a returnable boundary crossing: a large, negative-mean, high-variance, moderately persistent displacement concentrated around absences, after which the entity returns. Braxton et al. show the state-space version: the latent level continues through the absence and is re-anchored on return. Both treat absence as a gap in observation, not a death, which is the estimand the fact sheet says the exit machinery gets wrong (95–98% of empirical absences temporary).
- The direction of the DHM bias for the program is not obvious and should be measured, not assumed: gap-adjacent irregular observations inflate the difference-based permanent variance for everyone, while the simulation spreads that variance evenly as Gaussian home innovations instead of concentrating it in rare events. That combination could produce adequate average mobility together with too few large departures of established entities.
- This is distinct from the already-refuted proposal (missingness- or population-matched scoring as the explanation of the variance-ratio residual): the claim here concerns bias in estimation moments from gap-adjacent observations and selection in long differences, not the scoring population.
- The Sterk result that exit moves dispersion but not autocorrelations suggests a cheap triage: compare the program's change autocorrelations and its long-difference variances between a balanced, gap-free entity set and the full set. If autocorrelations agree and variances do not, selection acts on levels of dispersion (affecting sigma and D(h)) and not on dynamics (phi).
- The MP long-stayer check transfers directly as a standing robustness table.

### Gaps
- Fitzgerald, Gottschalk & Moffitt (1998) was not read; it appears only as cited by DHM and Moffitt–Gottschalk.
- The Abowd–Crepon–Kramarz–Trognon estimator's mechanics are unverified beyond the abstract.
- I found no source in this literature on a censored instrument of the Instagram kind (query-based thinning); the nearest analogues are top-coding (DHM impute a Pareto tail and report robustness to the method) and partial-period observation.
- The published AER version of Sterk et al. and its online appendix were not read; I read the conference draft.

## 7. Functional replacement of step profiles: parameterizing age/size profiles of variances and testing against unrestricted profiles

### Takeaway
The standard sequence is to estimate unrestricted profiles first, show them, then impose a low-order form (linear or quadratic, often in logs) and test it; flexible variance profiles are known to absorb misspecification in other components, which is a reason to restrict them before running over-identification tests.

### Cited Findings
- Karahan and Ozkan first estimate age profiles of persistence and shock variances with no functional form, identifying persistence from how covariances decay across lags at a given age, the persistent-shock variance from how covariances shift with age at a given lag, and the transitory variance as a remainder. They strongly reject flat profiles for persistence and persistent-shock variance, not for transitory variance, and find hump-shaped persistence and U-shaped persistent-shock variance. [AB] — [Karahan & Ozkan 2013, RED 16(3):452–476 (working paper)](https://economics.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/filevault/11-030.pdf)
- Moffitt and Gottschalk use a transitory innovation variance linear in age and, in the approximate method, a second-order polynomial in age and lag for log autocovariances; moments are binned into three age groups. [FT-s] — [Moffitt & Gottschalk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4297675)
- Braxton et al. specify log variances as linear-in-parameters functions of observables, with a quadratic in age as the worked example, estimated jointly with time effects inside EM. [FT] — [Braxton et al., NBER WP 29567](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29567/w29567.pdf)
- GKOS use a logistic function of age and the persistent state for shock probabilities and report having tried quadratic terms and age or income dependence in innovation variances; parsimony in parameters and state variables was an explicit design goal. [FT] — [GKOS SR 710](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr710.pdf)
- Blundell, Graber and Mogstad estimate age-specific variances by skill group on population data and report distinct shapes (rising permanent-shock variance with age for the low-skilled; large early permanent shocks declining to age 35 for the high-skilled; declining transitory variance for all). [AB] — [Blundell, Graber & Mogstad 2015](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1432915)
- Hoffmann shows that age variation in the covariance structure can be rationalized by age-dependent heteroskedasticity, so that estimates of another component (heterogeneous profiles) identified from that variation are biased. [AB] — [Hoffmann 2019, QE](https://mail.econometricsociety.org/publications/quantitative-economics/2019/07/01/HIP-RIP-and-the-robustness-of-empirical-earnings-processes/file/QE863.pdf)
- De Nardi et al. keep the profile fully nonparametric (age-specific transition matrices) and evaluate by comparison with the canonical process. [AB] — [De Nardi, Fella & Paz-Pardo 2020](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24326)
- Tests of restrictions in the passages I read are chi-square tests with bootstrap critical values or p-values (Meghir–Pistaferri, including equality of coefficients across groups) and quasi-likelihood-ratio tests for excluding parameters in simulated MD (DHM). [FT] — [Meghir & Pistaferri, WP version](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3862/1/3862.pdf); [DHM](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22938)

### Inferences
- The program has done the first step (unrestricted band profiles) thoroughly; the literature's second step is the missing one. The natural restricted forms are log-linear or log-quadratic in log permanent rank for each of sigma_perm, sigma_trans, sigma_obs and kappa, possibly with a monotonicity restriction, giving 8–12 coefficients in place of about 500 band values.
- Estimation should be one-step: stack all bands' moments and solve for the functional coefficients directly, so that the degrees of freedom of the distance test are (bands times moments per band) minus coefficients. Fitting curves through band-level NNLS estimates in a second stage discards that test and carries boundary-solution artefacts into the fit.
- The test statistic is the increase in the minimized distance from unrestricted to restricted, with critical values from the entity-and-week-block bootstrap; the reporting format is the GKOS ladder, one row per profile restriction with the objective decomposition and the out-of-sample gate.
- Hoffmann's point cuts in the program's direction: with fully flexible rank profiles, almost any rank variation in the covariance structure can be fitted, so in-sample scorecards have little power against misspecified dynamics. Restricted profiles would make the 15-row scorecard a sharper instrument and would make cross-platform comparison of a few coefficients possible (R7).
- Transfer limits: age is observed and exogenous in earnings data; permanent rank is estimated, endogenous to the process, and bounded by the universe cut-off, so profiles near the boundary are affected by selection (section 6) and should be tested for restriction separately from the interior.

### Gaps
- I did not find a paper in this literature that uses splines or shape-constrained estimation for variance profiles; the documented forms are polynomials, logistic functions, bins and fully nonparametric profiles.
- The Karahan–Ozkan test statistic and procedure are unverified beyond the abstract-level description.
- MaCurdy's work, the Meghir–Pistaferri handbook chapter (2011), Arellano's panel text and the Arellano–Bonhomme reviews were not retrieved.

## Implications for the rank-diffusion model

Ranked by expected value. Each item names the residual it addresses, the cheap measurement that should come first, and where it would not transfer. None of these changes a frozen spec or threshold; each is a candidate for pre-registration and owner review. All are my inferences from the cited findings.

**1. Gap-adjacent audit and levels-versus-differences concordance (DHM). Residuals: R1, R5; also the exit-estimand mismatch.**
- First measurement: an event-study regression of within-entity demeaned log activity (and its squared residual) on dummies for the three weeks before and after any absence, zero, entry or exit, by permanent-rank band; report mean shortfall, variance ratio, and the share of entity-weeks affected. Then recompute the per-band identifying moments (persistence-adjusted DHM forms) and the long-difference variances with those weeks trimmed.
- Decision rule to register: if trimming changes sigma_perm or D(h) by more than the bootstrap band in established bands, the current estimates are contaminated; if level-based and difference-based identifying moments disagree on the gap-free subsample, the dynamics are misspecified independently of gaps.
- If positive, the parsimonious model change is a boundary-displacement component (mean, variance, persistence; three parameters) estimated from the event study, with absence treated as a gap through which the home state continues.
- Limits: earnings gaps are mostly partial-year work; here zeros and absences in the deep tail may be instrument artefacts on censored platforms, so run census platforms first.

**2. Simulated minimum distance under the empirical presence rule. Residuals: R1, R3 (kappa), R5.**
- First measurement: run the existing estimator on panels simulated from the fitted model and passed through the empirical presence mask and universe rule, and report bias in each band value, especially kappa and D(52) (the Kaplan–Violante check). Separately compare empirical D(h) under pairwise-present, balanced, and floor-imputed rules.
- If bias is material, replace the analytic inversion for the affected parameters by matching simulated moments computed under identical conditioning (Sterk et al., GKOS, DHM section 4.3).
- Limits: loses the exact NNLS solve for those parameters; keep the analytic solution as the starting value and as a diagnostic.

**3. Weak-identification audit of kappa and the permanent/medium split. Residuals: R3, R6, R5.**
- First measurement: per band and per era, the singular values of the Jacobian of the moment map, the share of NNLS solutions at the zero bound, and a parametric-bootstrap sampling distribution of (kappa, sigma_perm) at the actual N and T. Compare the bootstrap spread of kappa with the observed 4–8x era shift.
- Follow-up: reparameterize to a well-identified pair (short-horizon variance slope and a long-horizon quantity), report confidence sets by inverting the distance statistic, exclude era-spanning long differences from era-specific fits, and test era equality on the well-identified combination only.
- Limits: Doris et al. studied a fixed effect versus an AR(1) with T of 8–25; the mapping to an OU home identified from long-difference curvature is by analogy.

**4. Composition-fixed decomposition of the s trend, then a dispersion loading. Residual: R3.**
- First measurement: quantiles (p10, p50, p90, p99) of per-entity change variance by era on a balanced, gap-trimmed entity set and by entry cohort, with sampling noise removed by a split-sample estimator; this separates composition, tail-driven change (Jensen–Shore) and a uniform widening.
- If a uniform widening survives, add one exponent per era on log amplitude and test it against the constant-s model by a distance-difference test with week-block bootstrap critical values. If the change is confined to the top decile, the right object is a small high-volatility type, not a loading.
- Limits: a common transitory loading is the already-refuted common volatility factor and is excluded. With three or four eras the trend has almost no time-series degrees of freedom; the Wikipedia confirmation is where a registered prediction about s can actually be tested.

**5. Horizon profile of quantile-based higher moments and conditional impulse responses. Residuals: R1, R2, R5.**
- First measurement: Kelley skewness and Crow–Siddiqui kurtosis of h-week log changes for h = 1, 4, 13, 52 by band, empirical versus simulated; and mean change from t-1 to t+k conditional on the size and sign of the change at t, by band, with fixed composition and conditioning on a lagged-window permanent rank.
- Reading: kurtosis that persists with horizon locates the missing large-displacement dynamics in a persistent or medium-timescale component, not the fast one (the GKOS finding for earnings); sign- or size-dependent reversion indicates nonlinear persistence. Either result directs the companion mechanism work and supplies untargeted validation statistics.
- Also replace the kurtosis-based degrees-of-freedom estimator with a quantile-based one and report both.
- Limits: this is measurement and validation, not the tail-shape surgery on fast innovations that has been refuted; adopting ABB's stochastic EM is not recommended at this panel length.

**6. Meghir–Pistaferri tests: heterogeneity versus ARCH, and one multiplier versus two. Residuals: R2, R1.**
- First measurement: pooled versus within-entity ARCH slopes for the transitory and permanent cross-product proxies, with week effects and block-bootstrap critical values; and a split-sample errors-in-variables regression of entity-level log permanent-shock variance on log transitory-shock variance.
- Reading: a surviving within-entity slope means volatility clustering that the persistent amplitude cannot represent and that inflates finite-window s; a disattenuated slope away from one, or correlation well below one, means b=1 needs a second multiplier.
- Limits: the proxies assume the fast component's memory is short relative to the window used; verify on simulated data from the fitted model first.

**7. Kiefer–Wolfowitz NPMLE of the amplitude distribution as a diagnostic. Residual: R2.**
- First measurement: NPMLE of the distribution of entity variances from band-standardized changes on each platform, compared with the fitted lognormal in the upper tail, plus the dependence of amplitude on home level within band.
- Adopt a replacement for the lognormal only if the diagnostic rejects and a one- or two-parameter family reproduces the NPMLE; otherwise record the lognormal as tested.
- Limits: the standard likelihood is Gaussian with a common AR coefficient; with Student-t shocks the sampling distribution of the entity statistic must be adjusted or a robust scale used, and the result should be checked for sensitivity to that choice.

**8. Measurement-error battery for the noise floor. Residuals: R5, R2.**
- First measurements: (a) first-order autocorrelation of weekly changes from weekly totals versus a fixed weekday's value (time aggregation); (b) odd-day versus even-day split-half series: autocovariance of their difference across weeks, its variance against level, its correlation with level; (c) scorecard, gate and head-law sensitivity to a noise variance of 0, 0.5, 1 and 1.5 times the floor.
- If (a) shows Working-type aggregation, add the implied MA term or fit the fast component on the daily clock; if (b) rejects whiteness, report the noise variance as a bounded interval, as MP do.
- Limits: a replication-based floor cannot detect error common to all days of a week (instrument eras, query censoring); that is a Kapteyn–Ypma-type problem needing a second instrument.

**9. One-step functional profiles with a distance test. Residuals: P, R7.**
- First measurement: fit log-linear and log-quadratic profiles in log permanent rank for each parameter in one stacked minimum-distance problem; report the distance increase over the unrestricted fit with bootstrap p-value, the in-sample scorecard and the out-of-sample gate for each rung.
- Limits: test boundary bands separately; rank is estimated and selected, unlike age.

**10. State-space quasi-likelihood cross-check with log-linear variances. Residuals: R4, R3, P.**
- First measurement: on one platform, estimate the same model by Kalman filter and EM with variances log-linear in rank and era, integrating the amplitude by one-dimensional quadrature; compare with the minimum-distance estimates and compute one-step predictive log score and CRPS.
- Value: two estimators with different sensitivities (Nakata–Tonetti; Braxton et al.); absences handled as missing data; a criterion aligned with proper scores, which second-moment matching is not.
- Limits: Gaussian quasi-likelihood is inefficient under Student-t shocks and the linear filter is not optimal; treat as a cross-check until validated on simulated data.

**11. Inference and reporting conventions. Residuals: all.**
- Keep identity weighting as primary; add a relative-deviation sensitivity run and, optionally, a cross-fitted regularized-weight run.
- Report entity-block and week-block bootstrap intervals for every band value and bootstrap p-values for distance statistics; treat era comparisons as time-series inference.
- Report a GKOS-style specification ladder with the objective decomposed by moment group, and publish the full targeted and untargeted moment set.
- Add a standing long-stayer robustness table (Meghir–Pistaferri) and a balanced-versus-unbalanced comparison of autocorrelations and variances (Sterk et al.).

**12. Harmonized model-free statistics across systems (GRID). Residual: R7.**
- First step: freeze one master script that computes, for any ranked panel, the item-5 statistics plus rank-mobility measures by permanent-rank band and horizon, and run it on existing platforms before Wikipedia contact so the Wikipedia mini-protocol can register predictions for them.
- Limits: descriptive breadth, not model confirmation; it lowers the cost of adding systems but does not substitute for the gate.
