# Task 2: the methods tradition of stochastic models of rank and size

## Objective

Review, across disciplines, the articles that use the kinds of stochastic models this draft relies on. Then answer three questions:
- What are these models good for?
- What do the strongest of these articles share in structure?
- What should our paper emulate?

Finally, suppose our model works as well as we hope and is modestly refined. What are the most interesting and important things it could reveal?

## Model families in scope

Treat these families, and others you judge closer, across:
- economics: firms, cities, wealth, income;
- finance;
- ecology and microbiology;
- cultural evolution;
- scientometrics;
- linguistics;
- physics;
- the study of online popularity.

The families:
- Random growth, or Gibrat's law, with a lower barrier or reflection (Champernowne, Kesten, Gabaix). Zipf's law as a balance of volatility and drift.
- Rank-based diffusions and stochastic portfolio theory: the Atlas model, first-order models, hybrid Atlas models. Capital-distribution curves and their stability.
- The speed of transitions in inequality (Gabaix, Lasry, Lions and Moll 2016). Type heterogeneity in growth.
- Size–variance scaling: the dependence of growth volatility on size, Taylor's law, fluctuation scaling.
- Neutral models and drift in ecology and cultural evolution, including turnover in top-y lists. Tests of neutrality.
- Preferential attachment, Yule–Simon processes, aging and fitness models of popularity, and rank-turnover models ("dynamics of ranking").
- Permanent and transitory decompositions in panel data, state-space or Kalman models, and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck reversion. Earnings dynamics with heterogeneous growth.
- Species-abundance turnover and stochastic logistic models in macroecology. "Chaotic" or persistent turnover.

Several are already cited; see `paper/references.bib`. Name the closest papers we do not cite, especially the closest predecessors and any recent competitor. Baker & Smyth 2026 is already cited as one competitor.

## Questions to answer

1. **What these models are good for.** List the scientific uses, each with its strongest example paper. For instance:
   - explaining a stationary distribution;
   - a counterfactual;
   - decomposing a change;
   - measuring a hidden parameter;
   - detecting departures from a null;
   - separating luck from lasting differences;
   - time scales;
   - forecasting;
   - measurement from partial observation.

   For which uses are they weak, and why?
2. **The anatomy of the strongest articles.** What structure recurs? Consider:
   - stylized facts;
   - a minimal model;
   - an analytic result;
   - calibration from independent moments;
   - novel or out-of-sample predictions;
   - null models;
   - comparisons across systems and data collapse;
   - dimensionless numbers or phase diagrams;
   - how failures are reported.

   Ground this in specific papers, with page-cited quotations where they make the point.
3. **What our draft is missing that it should emulate.**
   - Give at most about 8 concrete items, ranked.
   - For each: where in the draft it goes, what it would cost in space or analysis, and whether it needs new data. New data are mostly unavailable: CrowdTangle is closed, and the Reddit 2023–24 data are sealed for a confirmatory test.
4. **If the model works: the most interesting insights.**
   - Propose and rank the most important questions this model could answer, given the data on hand and modest refinement.
   - Rank by scientific importance, by feasibility, and by fit to a broad PNAS readership.
   - Some possible directions, to be judged and not assumed:
     - how long leaders stay;
     - first-passage times to the top;
     - mobility measures for attention, by analogy with income mobility;
     - how platform interventions would act as shocks to volatility or drift;
     - a dimensionless number for comparing platforms;
     - how fast attention inequality can change;
     - separating luck from persistent quality;
     - detecting entrenchment.
   - For each: state the analysis, what result would be interesting, and what could go wrong.
5. **Threats from this literature.** Which results, if a referee knows them, most threaten the paper's claims? For example, a known theorem that already implies a result presented as empirical. How should the draft pre-empt them?
