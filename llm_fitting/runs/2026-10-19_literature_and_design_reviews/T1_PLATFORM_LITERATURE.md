# Task 1: the substantive literature on digital platforms

## Objective

Find the most important scholarship on digital platforms that this draft does not cite, or engages only in passing. The focus is:
- platform dynamics;
- the attention economy;
- the most fundamental scholarly questions about these.

Then help the authors place the paper among the questions that literature asks.

**What we want is the few works that matter most, not hundreds.** A work matters if any of these is true:
- an editor or referee would expect it to be engaged;
- it changes how a claim should be stated;
- it supplies a stronger framing;
- it offers a result the paper should test against.

## Scope

**Fields:**
- computational social science;
- communication and political communication;
- information science;
- economics of platforms and of attention;
- sociology of culture and markets;
- HCI/CSCW research on online communities, including Reddit;
- platform governance and moderation (bans, quarantines, deplatforming);
- network science of online popularity.

**Period.** Emphasize 2018–2026. Include earlier work only if it is foundational, uncited, and something a referee would expect.

**Vocabulary.** Search with the vocabularies of neighbouring fields too, not only the draft's. For example:
- attention concentration and inequality;
- winner-take-all and superstar markets;
- the long tail;
- popularity dynamics and hit persistence;
- rank turnover and churn;
- audience fragmentation and audience duplication;
- online community life cycles, growth and decline;
- creator economy;
- platform power;
- algorithmic amplification;
- engagement inequality;
- cumulative advantage and the Matthew effect.

**Search both ways.** Look as hard for work that would weaken the paper's contribution as for work that supports it. Examples of weakening work:
- earlier findings of stable concentration alongside high churn online;
- demonstrations that platform changes, not intrinsic dynamics, drive such patterns.

## Deliverables

**A. Access check and search approach.** A short opening.

**B. Annotated bibliography.**
- Organize it by themes you choose, and explain the organization in two sentences.
- For each entry give:
  - the full citation with DOI or a stable URL;
  - the access label;
  - a priority tier:
    - **Tier 1, must engage:** a referee or editor would expect it, or it changes a claim. At most about 10.
    - **Tier 2, should cite.**
    - **Tier 3, useful background.**
  - an annotation, about 80–200 words, matched to the entry's importance. It should cover:
    - the work's core insight and evidence;
    - its relevance to a specific claim or section of our draft;
    - whether it supports, challenges, precedes, extends or qualifies that claim;
    - the actionable insight: what to cite where, what to rephrase, or what to test.
- Target 20–35 entries. Fewer is fine if that is what the literature supports.

**C. The questions the literature asks.** In four parts:
1. **The most common questions.** Which questions recur in recent published work on digital platforms and are relevant to research and models like ours?
2. **Meta-questions and implicit questions.** Which questions organize the field without being stated?
3. **Questions the literature is not asking but should.** Explain why each matters.
4. **Implied important questions.** Which questions follow from what the field does ask, even if no one states them?

For each question:
- Give 1–3 exact quotations, at most about 40 words each, with page numbers, showing where published scholars ask it or nearly ask it. Paraphrase the surrounding context in a sentence or two.
- Follow the quotation rules in the common brief strictly. An unverified quotation must be labelled. A labelled paraphrase is better than a quotation you cannot check.
- Say whether our draft answers the question, could answer it with the data on hand, or cannot.
- Say how the draft should frame its relation to the question.

**D. Synthesis for the draft.**
- The five most valuable changes to the paper's framing and positioning for a PNAS audience. Each should be specific and located: introduction, Discussion, or a particular claim.
- What the literature prevents us from claiming.
- Where the paper is more original than its current framing shows, if anywhere.

**E. Search log.**

## Length

About 6,000–10,000 words. Spend words where they carry the most value. Short entries for tier 3 are fine.
