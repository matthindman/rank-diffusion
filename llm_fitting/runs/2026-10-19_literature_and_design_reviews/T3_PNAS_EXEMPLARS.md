# Task 3: what the PNAS model papers teach about structure, tone and the preferences of editors and referees

## Objective

The owner collected 31 PNAS papers as models for this manuscript. They use stochastic, scaling or mechanistic models on empirical data, across many fields. They were collected to build a rough template, but have not been used well in drafting.

Analyze them for:
- structure;
- tone;
- writing style;
- the **implicit, revealed preferences of PNAS editors and referees**, beyond the formal guidelines.

Then compare them with our draft, and say concretely what to change to make the submission stronger.

## How to work

- **Read every exemplar's front matter**, meaning:
  - the title and the "Edited by" or "Contributed by" line;
  - the received and accepted dates, where printed;
  - the significance statement and abstract;
  - the introduction;
  - the first paragraph of each Results subsection;
  - the Discussion.
- **Read 6–10 in full.** Choose the ones closest to our paper in kind: a stochastic model of a ranked or size-distributed population, empirical, a broad readership. Say which you chose and why.
- **Count where a claim depends on frequency.** For example, how many of the 31 use claim-first Results headings. Do not impressionistically generalize.
- **Watch for survivorship bias.** You see only accepted papers. Separate what all of them share, which may simply be the PNAS format, from what distinguishes the papers most like ours.
- **Use outside evidence on editorial preferences where you can find it.** Examples: PNAS editorials or statements by editors on what they look for, guidance on desk rejection, or the Contributed versus Direct Submission tracks. Cite it with the quotation rules of the common brief.

## Deliverables

1. **Access check.**
2. **Structure.** Cover:
   - section architecture and the word budget of each section;
   - the main-text/SI split;
   - how many equations the main text carries and how a model is introduced;
   - the number of figures and the role of Fig. 1;
   - Results headings;
   - how null models and baselines appear;
   - where limitations go;
   - what goes in the main-text Methods.

   Give counts or ranges across the set, and name the papers that illustrate each pattern.
3. **Tone and style.** Cover:
   - sentence length and hedging;
   - first person;
   - how generality or universality is claimed;
   - how novelty is signalled without novelty claims;
   - questions as openers;
   - how specialist terms are introduced for a broad reader;
   - how failures and rival models are written about.

   Include a few short exemplary passages: exact quotations with page numbers, verified against the files.
4. **Revealed preferences of editors and referees.**
   - What these papers jointly reveal that the formal guidelines do not say. Possible signals:
     - which handling editors and fields appear;
     - the submission track;
     - time to acceptance;
     - what every paper has, for example a cross-system comparison or an analytic result checked by simulation;
     - what none has.
   - Separate evidence from inference.
5. **Comparison with our draft.**
   - Where the draft already matches the best exemplars.
   - Where it departs, at the level of the title, significance statement, abstract, introduction, each Results subsection, figures, Discussion and Methods.
   - A ranked list of concrete edits, with locations.
   - What to move to the SI to reach 6–7 pages, or a reasoned case for staying at 8.
6. **Templates to follow.**
   - The 3–5 exemplars that should serve as structural models, and why.
   - A skeleton of our paper mapped onto them, with a word budget for each section and a role for each figure.
7. **What the earlier template attempts got right and wrong**, given the paper's current question.
